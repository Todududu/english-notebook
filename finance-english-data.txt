[PASSAGE 3 | What accountants and auditors do]
Accounting is more than bookkeeping. A bookkeeper records transactions every day, but accountants also prepare financial statements for shareholders and creditors. This is financial accounting. Inside the company, managers use the same data to make plans and decisions. This is called management accounting. Once a year, independent auditors carry out an external audit. They check the company's systems of control and look for errors or possible fraud. They also make sure there is no creative accounting, such as showing an artificially high profit. Finally, they confirm that the accounts give a true and fair view of the company's financial situation and follow the accounting standards.
Q: Who carries out an external audit? | the managing director | internal auditors | *independent auditors | the tax authorities
Q: What is management accounting used for? | *making plans and decisions | paying tax | finding fraud | selling shares

[PASSAGE 4 | A day in the books]
Here is a simple example of double-entry bookkeeping. A furniture company buys wood, a raw material, and agrees to pay the supplier in thirty days. The bookkeeper debits the purchases account and credits the supplier's account. A week later, the company sells a table on credit. The bookkeeper credits the sales account and debits the customer's account, because the customer is now one of the company's debtors. The table leaves the warehouse, so the level of stock goes down. Sales are first written in a day book, and a weekly summary goes into the nominal ledger. At the end of the accounting period, the bookkeeper prepares a trial balance. If everything is correct, total debits equal total credits.
Q: When a customer buys on credit, which account is debited? | the sales account | *the customer's account | the supplier's account | the purchases account
Q: What is prepared at the end of the accounting period? | a day book | *a trial balance | a budget | an interim report

[PASSAGE 5 | From sole trader to company]

[PASSAGE 6 | Reading a company report]
In Britain, most companies are private companies with 'Ltd' after their name. They cannot sell their shares on an open market. Readers usually look first at turnover, then at gross profit, which is turnover less cost of sales, and finally at net profit, after administrative expenses and tax.

[PASSAGE 7 | Choosing accounting policies]
Companies can choose their accounting policies, but they must follow the rules. In the United States, companies use GAAP. In most other countries they use IFRS. Whatever method a company chooses, it must be consistent and use the same method every year. If it changes a policy, it must disclose the change in the annual report. Policies on depreciation, the valuation of inventory and provisions can make a big difference to profit. Most countries use the historical cost principle, so assets stay at their original purchase price. This makes sense for a going concern, because it is not planning to sell its assets. In countries with high inflation, however, some companies use replacement cost accounting instead.

Q: Why is historical cost acceptable for a going concern? | *it is not planning to sell its assets | prices never change | it pays no tax | it has no inventory

[PASSAGE 8 | The rules behind the numbers]

Finally, when there is a choice of methods, the company chooses the more careful one. This is conservatism.

[PASSAGE 11 | Two halves that balance]
A balance sheet is a picture of a company on one day. One half shows its assets, such as factories, machines and cash. Shareholders' equity includes share capital and retained earnings, and it is the same as the company's net assets. But the balance sheet does not show how much money came in or went out during the year. For that, you need the profit and loss account and the cash flow statement.

[PASSAGE 20 | How banks make money]
When people deposit money in a commercial bank, the bank pays them a little interest. It then lends most of that money to borrowers at a higher rate. The difference is the bank's profit. Banks must keep a percentage of deposits as reserves. With a 10% reserve requirement, a deposit of 100 million dong allows a loan of 90 million. When that money is spent and deposited in another bank, it can be lent again. In this way, the banking system creates credit. Before lending, banks carry out a risk assessment: riskier loans pay a higher yield.
